You change a phrase in the system prompt. The next answer looks better. Was it the change, or was it chance?

Without measurement that question cannot be answered. **Evals** are the answer: a fixed set of test cases with a defined scoring method, against which every change is run.

## Why trying it out is not enough

With ordinary software you check changes with tests, because behaviour is deterministic. With models that goes away:

- **The same input gives different outputs.** A single run tells you little.
- **Improvements in one place break others.** A prompt that fixes case A often breaks case B — and you only notice if you still test B.
- **Your own impression deceives.** After a change you check exactly the cases you made it for. That is not a sample.

<Callout type="merke">
Without evals you optimise on anecdotes. That works for a while and then becomes unreliable, without you noticing when it tipped.
</Callout>

## How to start

Getting started is easier than the term suggests.

**Collect cases, do not invent them.** Twenty real inputs from actual use are worth more than a hundred made-up ones. Especially valuable: the cases that went wrong. If you are not in production yet, collect while playing through manually.

**Start small.** Twenty cases is a usable start. Two is too few to separate noise from effect; waiting for a thousand means never starting.

**Define the scoring.** For each case it must be clear what "correct" means. Roughly three kinds:

| Kind | Scoring | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| exact | string or value comparison | classification, extraction, IDs |
| programmatic | code checks the output | valid JSON, tests pass, schema satisfied |
| judgemental | a human or model rates it | summaries, tone, explanations |

The first two are cheap and reliable. The third is more expensive and less stable — shift as much as possible into the first two.

## LLM-as-judge, with care

For soft criteria you have a model do the rating. That is workable but has quirks worth knowing:

- **Concrete criteria rather than grades.** "Does the answer name all three required points?" produces steadier judgements than "rate the quality from 1 to 10".
- **Position effects.** When comparing two answers, models systematically favour one position. If you compare, swap the order and average.
- **The judge needs checking too.** A handful of hand-scored cases shows whether the rating agrees with human judgement at all. An uncalibrated judge reliably measures the wrong thing.

## What to measure alongside

Quality is not the only quantity that shifts while optimising:

- **Cost per case** — otherwise you optimise quality up and the invoice with it
- **Runtime** — especially for interactive applications
- **Failure rate** — aborted runs, invalid outputs, loops
- **Variance** — how much the result fluctuates across repeated runs of the same case

The last is the most commonly forgotten. A setup that is good on average and occasionally very bad is often more painful in production than one that is consistently slightly weaker.

<Callout type="praxis">
Evals are most useful not as a grand quality measurement but as **regression protection**. Every case that was once wrong goes into the collection. Then the next rebuild tells you immediately if it breaks again. That collection grows on its own and after a few months is the most valuable artefact in the whole project.
</Callout>

## The connection to everything else

Almost everything in this tutorial is a change whose effect you should measure: a different model, a reworked prompt, fewer active [MCP servers](../mcp/), a different split across [sub-agents](../multi-agent/), an extra verification stage against [hallucinations](../hallucinations/).

Without evals those are all guesses. With evals they are decisions.

<KeyTakeaway>
- Models are not deterministic — single runs and personal impressions do not count as evidence.
- Twenty real cases with clear scoring is a usable start; failed cases are the most valuable.
- Exact and programmatic scoring are steadier and cheaper than having a model judge.
- Alongside quality, track cost, runtime and variance — and the biggest payoff is regression protection.
</KeyTakeaway>
